Executive Summary 
 
This is Volume I of a three-volume report.   
In the modernized National Spatial Reference System (NSRS) the National Geodetic Survey 
(NGS) will have new tools for external users of the NSRS (such as a revised version of the 
Online Positioning User Service, OPUS) as well as for internal computations of new NSRS 
coordinates called reference epoch coordinates (RECs) and survey epoch coordinates (SECs).  
Each of these three applications (OPUS, REC and SEC) will use a least-squares adjustment 
(LSA) to estimate coordinates (and their associated variances).  But each LSA will be faced with 
the same basic problem – observations are made at a variety of epochs (dates and times) while 
estimated coordinates are required at one specific epoch.  This is further complicated by the 
possibility that both stochastic constraints and fixed constraints are imposed on the parameters, 
and that they may be imposed at a variety of additional epochs.   
The estimation of parameters at one epoch, from observations and constraints given at other 
epochs will be called the multi-epoch least-squares adjustment (ME-LSA) problem. 
This report derives the equations that relate observations and constraints, provided at multiple 
epochs, to estimable parameters (usually geodetic coordinates plus some nuisance parameters) at 
a different epoch.  The derivations are generic, applicable to any ME-LSA problem, though 
certain geodetic-specific situations will be addressed.  Solutions to those equations are not found 
herein, since the particular least-squares solution (LESS) depends on a variety of factors (such as 
rank deficiency of the model and the number of estimated variance components).  However, 
references to where such solutions can be found are provided for all but one case, where the 
existence of a solution remains hypothetical. 
The key factor which allows the estimation of parameters at one epoch from observations made, 
and constraints imposed, at various other epochs is a model of changes in parameter values 
(MCPV).   
While the MCPV is key to modeling and solving the ME-LSA problem, there are multiple ways 
it can be used to do so.  The most common method used by NGS is a method that we will call the 
projection method.  That is the method derived herein, where we will differentiate between 
observations (which were made at various epochs) and projected observations (“moved through 
time” or “projected” to the adjustment epoch).  A similar distinction is made between constraints 
and projected constraints.   
There are other methods possible, but not as widely used.  Another, that we will call the direct 
method has been developed at NGS, which does not rely upon projected observations and 
constraints.  That method is detailed in Volume II of this report.  As a matter of interest, despite 
their different approaches to modeling the ME-LSA problem, it may be proven that the 
projection method and the direct method are, at their core, mathematically identical.  That proof 
will be provided in Volume III of this report, as well as a discussion of other methods of

Notation and Terminology 
 
The complexity of this report has required that very specific conventions be followed so that 
variables can be unique, meaningful and understood at a glance.  We have chosen to follow, as 
best we could, certain conventions popularized by Burkhard Schaffrin (Snow, 2021) as follows: 
• Bold, Latin, upper-case (e.g. 𝑾𝑾, 𝒀𝒀) represent vectors of complete (i.e., non-linearized) 
random values 
• Bold, Latin, lower-case (e.g. 𝒘𝒘, 𝒚𝒚) represent vectors of incremental (i.e., possibly 
linearized) random values 
• Non-bold, Latin, upper-case (e.g. 𝐴𝐴, 𝐹𝐹, 𝑆𝑆) represent matrices 
• Non-bold, Latin, lower-case (e.g. 𝑎𝑎, 𝑓𝑓, 𝑖𝑖, 𝑗𝑗) represent functions or general variables 
• Bold, Greek, upper-case (e.g. 𝚵𝚵, 𝚿𝚿) represent vectors of complete, non-random values 
• Bold, Greek, lower-case (e.g. 𝝃𝝃, 𝜸𝜸) represent vectors of incremental non-random 
values 
• Non-bold, Greek, upper-case (e.g. Φ, Λ) and lower-case (e.g. 𝛼𝛼, 𝛽𝛽) are rarely used and 
their meaning will be explained when necessary 
• An overbar (e.g. 𝒀𝒀�, 𝑆𝑆̅, 𝒚𝒚�, 𝒆𝒆𝒚𝒚�) represents something projected (i.e., in time) or related 
to projected data 
• A lack of subscript on vector 𝚵𝚵 means “at the adjustment epoch” 
• A lack of subscript on other vectors means “a combination of many sub-vectors at 
their respective epochs” 
• The sum of a random and non-random quantity is always random

The words “mapped onto other values” appear repeatedly, which requires some explanation.  
The MCPV provides direct access to changes in parameter values over time, as well as their 
random errors.  When discussing errors provided directly from the MCPV, variables will have a 
capital 𝑀𝑀 subscript.  However, to compute changes to observations (or constraints) in time, the 
MCPV (including its random errors) must be “mapped” onto observations (or constraints) using 
appropriate functions.  When discussing the MCPV random errors that have been so-mapped 
onto observations or constraints, variables will have a lower-case 𝑚𝑚 subscript.   
Additionally, the order of subscripts, particularly with respect to the 𝑚𝑚 subscript is intentional, 
as it implies a way of reading the variable name.  Consider, for example, the difference between 
random-error vectors 𝒆𝒆𝒚𝒚� and 𝒆𝒆𝒚𝒚�,𝒎𝒎.  The first, with a single subscript, tells us that this vector 
contains all of the random errors in the incremental, projected, observation vector, 𝒚𝒚�.  But the 
second, with two subscripts, tells us that this vector only contributes to 𝒆𝒆𝒚𝒚�, and that the source of 
the contribution is the MCPV.

1 Introduction 
As part of the modernized National Spatial Reference System (NSRS), the National Geodetic 
Survey (NGS) is developing new surveying specifications, new models for the gravity field, for 
motions of geodetic marks (which we will call simply “marks”) and other phenomena, new 
definitions of coordinates and an entirely new Online Positioning User Service (OPUS) software 
suite.  Central to many of those changes is the task of estimating coordinates at a specific epoch 
of interest through the least-squares adjustment (LSA) of observations made in geodetic surveys 
at various epochs.  These LSAs will typically involve a combination of observations, prior 
information and constraints on the unknown parameters. 
The estimation of parameters at one epoch, from observations and constraints given at other 
epochs will be called the multi-epoch least-squares adjustment (ME-LSA) problem. 
One critical assumption is made in both Volume I and Volume II of this report:  that the 
parameters being estimated only include coordinates and nuisance parameters.  No velocities, 
displacements or other phenomena are contained within the estimable parameter set.  However, 
alternative modeling of the ME-LSA problem, including the estimation of such phenomena, will 
be briefly addressed in Volume III. 
The need to consider epochs in time is motivated by the dynamics of the earth’s crust to which 
marks are attached.  To accommodate those physical motions, it is assumed that one or more 
geodetic-value change-models3,4 (GVCMs) are available.  This has been the case for years at 
NGS with the Horizontal Time-Dependent Positioning (HTDP) model.  However, that model 
will be expanded to include ellipsoidal heights and new GVCMs that estimate changes to the 
geoid and gravity field will soon be available.  We will therefore generically refer to a model of 
changes in parameter values or MCPV to describe the change to any estimable parameter 
(whether it is a coordinate or not) over time.  The MCPV can be treated as known without 
variance (fixed) or known with variance (stochastic).  Both approaches are outlined herein.5 
The purpose of this report is to present the mathematical equations needed for any of these 
parameter-estimating LSAs, without regard to any specific type of network adjustment.  
Addressing specific adjustments will be done in future reports. 
However, the problem of estimating unknown model parameters at one epoch which rely upon 
observations at one or more different epochs can be handled in more than one way.  The method 
outlined in this report will be called the projection method.  That method relies upon projecting

observations and constraints from their given epochs to the adjustment epoch, to derive projected 
observations and projected constraints.  Other methods exist.  One, which we call the direct 
method, does not rely upon projecting anything through time, but rather builds a direct 
relationship between observations and constraints at their respective epochs and the unknown 
parameters at the adjustment epoch.  The direct method is derived in Volume II of this report, 
and though it takes a different mathematical approach to the ME-LSA, it can be shown to have 
mathematical equivalence to the projection method.  The proof of their equivalence will be 
provided in Volume III.   
While LSAs have a variety of wider uses within NGS, such as estimating tropospheric 
parameters and integer ambiguities in baseline processing of Global Navigation Satellite 
Systems, (GNSS) observables, those uses are not addressed herein.  This report restricts itself 
solely to a limited list of geodetic observations, prior information and constraints as input and 
estimated geodetic coordinates and some nuisance parameters, if necessary, as output (i.e., 
network adjustments).  The central question is:  how should the model be constructed when 
observations and constraints come from a variety of epochs, while parameters are estimated at a 
common epoch and the MCPV might contain random errors? 
This report contains the following sections: 
Section 2 contains a discussion of parameters that change over time, and how they are related to 
the parameters of interest (i.e., at the adjustment epoch). 
Sections 3, 4 and 5 describe the general equations for observations, stochastic constraints and 
fixed constraints (on the unknown parameters), respectively.  Formulae for transforming the 
observations and constraints to projected values at the adjustment epoch are provided, under the 
cases of the MCPV being fixed or stochastic.  Linearization of the observation and constraint 
equations is described, leading to incremental equations required to solve the ME-LSA problem 
iteratively. 
Section 6 describes the stochastic modeling of the various random errors. 
Section 7 combines the equations from sections 3, 4, 5 and 6.  The ME-LSA is then further 
formulated as either the Gauss-Markov Model (GMM) or the Variance Component Model

A primary goal of many least-squares adjustments is to estimate unknown parameters of interest, 
from observations that have a specified relationship to those parameters.  In geodetic 
applications, parameters may include a variety of quantities but they frequently contain

coordinates of marks, such as global Cartesian coordinates, orthometric heights or acceleration of 
gravity, though so-called “nuisance” parameters, such as orientation of a set of directions, may 
also need to be estimated.  Observations that connect marks include GNSS vectors, angles, 
distances, leveled height differences and more.  Although the mathematics herein can be applied 
to any least-squares adjustment problem where parameter values change over time, we will tend 
to focus solely on those quantities common to geodesy, and in particular the estimation of 
geodetic coordinates. 
Because marks are physical objects attached to the Earth’s crust, and because the crust moves 
and the gravity field changes over time, the geodetic coordinates of points tend to vary over time.  
This means observations connecting such points should also be expected to vary, depending on 
when they were made.  As an example, an observation of a GNSS baseline between two points in 
2000 might reasonably be expected to differ from an observation between the same two points 
made in 2020, due to crustal motion.

3.2 Addressing changes in parameter values in time 
Although a number of solutions may be proposed, we hypothesize four options to deal with the 
inclusion of 𝚫𝚫𝚵𝚵𝒊𝒊, each with its pros and cons:  
1. Leave all the vectors 𝚵𝚵 and 𝚫𝚫𝚵𝚵𝒊𝒊 on the right side of (7) to be estimated. 
2. Assume that all the changes to parameter values in 𝚫𝚫𝚵𝚵𝒊𝒊 are the result of linear changes in 
time (velocities; call them 𝚵𝚵̇ ) and estimate both 𝚵𝚵 and 𝚵𝚵̇ . 
3. Use a model of changes in parameter values (MCPV) to provide approximations to the 
vectors 𝚫𝚫𝚵𝚵𝒊𝒊.  These can then be used to “project” observations (and constraints) through 
time.  This option moves 𝚫𝚫𝚵𝚵𝒊𝒊 from the right side to the left side of the observation 
equation. 
4. Like option 3, use an MCPV to provide approximations to the quantities 𝚫𝚫𝚵𝚵𝒊𝒊, but unlike 
option 3, leave the observations at their observation epochs (e.g. do not create projected 
observations), and leave constraints at their constraint epochs (e.g. no projected 
constraints), but use 𝚫𝚫𝚵𝚵𝒊𝒊 to modify the design matrix, 𝐴𝐴, etc., and estimate 𝚵𝚵 directly 
from the non-projected observations and constraints.  This option also moves 𝚫𝚫𝚵𝚵𝒊𝒊 from

Option 1, while mathematically conceivable, is computationally impractical.  A proof-by-

Option 2 assumes linear motion for all parameters.  While this option has fewer unknown 
parameters than option 1, the assumption of linear change cannot be applied in general, since 
events such as earthquakes can invalidate the assumption of linear change to a coordinate 
(parameter).  Also, doubling the number of parameters to be estimated (from 𝚵𝚵 to 𝚵𝚵 and 𝚵𝚵̇ )

maintains a database of over 2 million points, of which only about 5 percent have ever been 
surveyed more than one time.) 
That leaves just options 3 and 4.  Option 4 will be called the direct method, and it might be 
considered a “purer” option, in that it does not rely upon projected observations.  On the other 
hand, it is a method not generally found in geodetic literature, and requires a bit of extra 
bookkeeping to implement.  Furthermore, though options 3 and 4 are formulated differently, they 
are strongly related, and it can be proven that the estimates of parameter values in each method 
are identical.  This proof will be provided in Volume III.  So, while option 4 is viable, it is not 
well known to the geodetic community.  It is, however, mathematically interesting.  As such, 
Volume II of this report will provide the details of the direct method, but NGS is not expected to 
pursue that path when solving the ME-LSA problem in future applications.

5.2.2 When the model of changes in parameter values is stochastic 
When the constraints are fixed, but the MCPV is stochastic, different considerations must be 
made depending on whether the fixed-constraint epoch is the same as the adjustment epoch.  If 
the fixed-constraint epoch is the same as the adjustment epoch, then the MCPV is not needed to 
project the fixed constraints in time.  In this case, the stochastic nature of the MCPV is irrelevant, 
and the constraint equation remains a fixed-constraint equation.  However, if the fixed-constraint 
epoch falls at any other epoch besides the adjustment epoch, then the random errors in the 
MCPV mean that the constraint equation must be written as a stochastic-constraint equation. 
Because these two special cases are mutually exclusive, they will be addressed separately and 
then combined later. 
To facilitate the discussion, we will use a subscripted variable 𝑡𝑡 to denote the times of particular 
epochs.  We therefore introduce the following four variables (where 𝑝𝑝 is the number of 
observation epochs and 𝑞𝑞 is the number of stochastic-constraint epochs). 
𝑡𝑡𝑖𝑖 denotes observation epoch 𝑖𝑖; 
𝑡𝑡𝑝𝑝+𝑗𝑗 denotes stochastic-constraint epoch 𝑗𝑗; 
𝑡𝑡𝑝𝑝+𝑞𝑞+𝑘𝑘 denotes fixed-constraint epoch 𝑘𝑘, and

8 Solving the multi-epoch least-squares adjustment problem 
So far, we have only described how to model the ME-LSA using the projection method.  We 
have not provided a solution to the ME-LSA problem.   
Least-squares solutions to the ME-LSA problem exist within the following three models: 
• The Gauss-Markov Model (GMM) with a fixed MCPV 
• The Gauss-Markov Model (GMM) with a stochastic MCPV 
• The Variance Component Model (VCM) with a fixed MCPV 
Within the GMM, with or without a stochastic MCPV, least-squares solutions for the unknown 
model parameters are found in Snow (2021), chapters 3 (GMM without constraints), 5 (GMM 
with fixed constraints) and 6 (GMM with stochastic constraints).  For the GMM with both fixed 
and stochastic constraints, a solution is found in Snow and Smith (2023). 
For the VCM, with or without constraints but only with a fixed MCPV, the solution is provided 
in Snow (2021), chapter 16.   
Within the VCM with a stochastic MCPV, the existence of a solution is not guaranteed, as 
mentioned in section 7.2.2.  Some initial experiments point to some numerical stability issues in 
this case, necessitating further investigation.  However, considering the cause of the potential

9 Future Work 
This report is Volume I of a 3-part report dealing with the multi-epoch least-squares adjustment 
(ME-LSA) problem, which consists of estimating parameters at one epoch from observations, 
stochastic constraints and fixed constraints at other epochs.  It outlined the mathematical model 
and procedures necessary to estimate those parameters using a technique which we have called 
the “projection method”.  That method involves transforming the original observations and 
constraints from their various epochs into projected observations and constraints, where the 
projected values refer to the adjustment epoch. 
There is another method of modeling the ME-LSA problem, which we call the “direct method”.  
That approach has a great number of similarities to, but subtle differences from, the approach in 
this report. The key difference is that there are no projected values.  A relationship is developed 
directly between observations and constraints at their various epochs and the parameters at the 
adjustment epoch.  That method is discussed in Volume II.  Despite their different approaches to 
modeling the ME-LSA problem, it can be shown that the two methods are actually 
mathematically identical.  A proof of that equivalence will be in Volume III of this report.  A 
graphical representation of the two approaches, including the basic notational differences, is 
shown in Figure 4.

Appendix A:  Details of the random errors with emphasis on the model 
of changes in parameter values 
The stochastic model of changes in parameter values (MCPV) introduces a complexity in 
modeling of the random errors that needs careful consideration.  The details are a bit lengthy, so 
they are provided in this appendix rather than the main body of the report.  Because they are so 
numerous and their consideration will radically impact the number of computations in the 
ME-LSA problem, we begin with a detailed discussion of the covariances within a stochastic 
MCPV.   
However, understanding the covariances in a stochastic MCPV begins with an understanding of 
the current state of geodetic value change models (GVCMs), such as the horizontal time-
dependent positioning (HTDP) model and their relationship to the MCPV. 
 
A.1 Relation between geodetic-value change-models and the model of changes to 
parameter values 
Currently, the best source within NGS for deriving changes in parameter values are one or more 
geodetic-value change-models (GVCMs).  Such models, what they contain, and their status are 
listed below: 
• HTDP (available):  Grids of linear velocities in local East, North (and some Up).  Grids 
of linear displacements (due to earthquakes) in local East, North (and some Up).  No 
stochastic information. 
• DGEOID2022 (available in beta form):  Grids of linear velocities of geoid undulations, 
and grids of the standard deviations of those velocities. 
• IFDM2022 (an alpha form is being tested in NGS; a public beta release is expected in 
2024):  Operates much like HTDP, and will replace it, but has grids of standard 
deviations of velocities but none for displacements. 
If the unknown parameters in the ME-LSA problem are global Cartesian coordinates 𝑋𝑋, 𝑌𝑌, 𝑍𝑍, 
then the MCPV will be changes in 𝑋𝑋, 𝑌𝑌, 𝑍𝑍 between two epochs.  In order for HTDP to provide 
the MCPV, the gridded linear velocities would need to be multiplied by time, and the entire 
𝐸𝐸, 𝑁𝑁, 𝑈𝑈 frame rotated to align with the 𝑋𝑋, 𝑌𝑌, 𝑍𝑍 frame.  This is why it was stated in the introduction 
of this report that GVCMs, such as HTDP, are not the MCPV, but rather provide the information 
needed to compute the MCPV.  This situation actually causes some significant complexity when 
attempting to transform the stochastic information of, say, IFDM2022 into the dispersion matrix 
of the MCPV.  That will mostly be covered in another paper.  It is sufficient to state that, if 
stochastic information is available in the GVCM, that it is available in the MCPV.  However, 
three immediate conclusions can be reached if the above GVCMs remain the source for the 
MCPV: 
1) When the unknown parameters are global Cartesian coordinates 𝑋𝑋, 𝑌𝑌, 𝑍𝑍, then error 
covariances between coordinate types, say 𝑋𝑋 and 𝑌𝑌, at the same point will always exist, 

##########################################################################
### PDF page 55 (of 68)   matches: epoch
##########################################################################
43 
 
even if they did not exist between coordinate types in the GVCM (say between East and 
North).  This is due to the full rotation matrix that transforms changes in East, North and 
Up into changes in 𝑋𝑋, 𝑌𝑌 and 𝑍𝑍. 
2) When the GVCM contains grids of linear velocities, error covariances between epochs 
(say 𝑖𝑖 = 1 and 𝑖𝑖 = 2) at the same point will always exist.  This is because the changes in 
a parameter, from epoch 𝑖𝑖 = 1 to the adjustment epoch are computed by multiplying the 
linear velocity of that parameter times Δ𝑡𝑡1, whereas the changes in a parameter, from 
epoch 𝑖𝑖 = 2 to the adjustment epoch are computed by multiplying that same linear 
velocity times Δ𝑡𝑡2.  If that linear velocity has a variance, then this use of a common 
velocity immediately means there will be a covariance between the two epochs in the 
MCPV.   
3) With no covariance information provided between the gridded values (whether velocities 
or displacements), it is difficult, maybe impossible, to accurately derive covariances 
between two different points.  Therefore, at this time, it seems unlikely that error 
covariances between points (say 𝐴𝐴 and 𝐵𝐵) will exist in the MCPV, unless those points are 
so close to one another that they rely on one or more of the same gridded velocities from 
the GVCM. 
Further details of the relationship between one or more GVCMs and the MCPV are deferred for 
future papers.

To summarize, in performing multi-epoch least-squares adjustment computations, practical 
considerations imply that four types of covariances should be ignored, regardless of whether they 
are known: 
1. Covariances between epochs 
2. Covariances between observations and the MCPV 
3. Covariances between stochastic constraints and the MCPV 
4. Covariances between observations and stochastic constraints
